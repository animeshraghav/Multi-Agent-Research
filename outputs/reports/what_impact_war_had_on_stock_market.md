# what impact war had on stock market?

# Introduction

Geopolitical conflicts and military invasions invariably generate significant uncertainty across global financial systems. When war breaks out, financial markets react swiftly to shifting expectations regarding global economic stability, trade disruptions, inflation, and corporate earnings. While the immediate reaction of stock markets to geopolitical shocks is characterized by sell-offs and heightened volatility, historical data demonstrates a pattern of long-term market resilience. This report examines the short-term disruptions, long-term performance trends, and key underlying economic drivers that shape stock market behavior during periods of war.

---

# Key Findings

### Short-Term Volatility and Initial Sell-Offs

* **Immediate Market Reaction:** Military conflicts and unprovoked invasions trigger immediate negative abnormal returns and sharp increases in market volatility as investors seek liquidity and reduce risk exposure.
* **Average Drawdown and Recovery Timeline:** Quantitative event studies conducted by financial research institutions (including LPL Research and AllianceBernstein) reveal a consistent historical pattern for major geopolitical shock events:
  * **Day 1 Impact:** The S&P 500 typically experiences an initial decline of approximately **1.0% to 1.1%** on the day of the event.
  * **Trough:** Markets reach a local bottom averaging **-4.7% to -5.0%**, taking approximately **19 to 22 days** to reach the trough.
  * **Recovery:** The index full recovers its pre-conflict levels within an average of **40 to 43 days**.
* **Forward-Looking Volatility Metrics:** Academic studies evaluating text-based war discourse indices indicate that heightened public and media attention surrounding geopolitical risks can predict elevated stock market volatility up to **12 months ahead**.

### Long-Term Resilience and Performance

* **Wartime Historical Performance:** A long-term study by the CFA Institute analyzing major military conflicts since 1926 indicates that U.S. equities have generated positive average returns over the duration of wartime periods.
* **12-Month Post-Conflict Returns:** Historical data shows that 12 months after the onset of a major conflict, the S&P 500 historically posts positive gains on average:
  * **AllianceBernstein:** Estimates an average 12-month return of approximately **+7.0%**.
  * **Stock Trader's Almanac:** Estimates an average 12-month return of approximately **+2.92%**.
* **Case Examples:**
  * **Russia-Ukraine Conflict (February 2022):** One year following the invasion, the S&P 500 was down roughly 6%. However, this drawdown was predominantly driven by macroeconomic factors, including surging global inflation and rapid monetary tightening by the Federal Reserve. Over the subsequent multi-year period, the S&P 500 rebounded by over **60%**.
  * **Gaza Conflict (October 2023):** One year after the outbreak of the conflict, the S&P 500 posted a gain exceeding **30%**.

### Primary Market Drivers and Structural Differences

* **Corporate Earnings vs. Geopolitical Events:** Major financial institutions, including Fidelity and Goldman Sachs, emphasize that long-term equity performance is governed primarily by macro economic fundamentals and corporate earnings growth rather than isolated military conflicts. Wars significantly alter long-term equity trajectories only when they directly disrupt core global economic mechanisms, such as critical energy supply shocks (e.g., the 1973 Arab oil embargo).
* **Advanced vs. Emerging Markets:** Empirical research reveals a divergence in market vulnerability: emerging market stock indices demonstrate higher sensitivity and stronger negative price reactions to international conflicts compared to advanced market equities.
* **Sector-Specific Disparities:** Military conflicts trigger significant sector rotation. European defense and aerospace equities, for instance, frequently attract substantial capital inflows as sovereign governments expand defense budgets and scale up long-term military procurement.

---

# Analysis

The empirical evidence demonstrates that equity markets operate as forward-looking discounting mechanisms. The primary mechanism driving short-term declines during a geopolitical crisis is the sudden surge in financial uncertainty, which leads investors to demand a higher risk premium. Consequently, initial sell-offs are often panic-driven risk reductions rather than precise re-pricings of long-term corporate earnings capabilities.

Once the initial scope of a conflict becomes defined and the immediate uncertainty subsides, markets re-focus on baseline macroeconomic variables: interest rates, consumer demand, corporate profitability, and fiscal policy. Unless a conflict induces systemic, structural damage to global trade routes or essential commodity supplies (such as crude oil or agricultural products), corporate earnings generally adapt, allowing equities to resume their underlying trajectory. Furthermore, increased state expenditures during wartime—such as expanded defense procurement and infrastructure spending—can act as localized fiscal stimuli, supporting earnings in specific industrial sectors.

---

# Conclusion

While the onset of war introduces sharp short-term sell-offs and elevated market volatility, historical evidence illustrates that equity markets exhibit remarkable resilience over broader investment horizons. On average, major market indices bottom within three weeks of a conflict outbreak and recover their losses within six weeks. Over 12-month and multi-year horizons, stock market trajectories remain predominantly tied to core macroeconomic conditions and corporate earnings rather than geopolitical events alone.
